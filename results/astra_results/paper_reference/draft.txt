SATzilla: Portfolio-based Algorithm Selection for SAT
LIN XU∗ , University of British Columbia, Canada
FRANK HUTTER, University of British Columbia, Canada
HOLGER H. HOOS, University of British Columbia, Canada
Background: It has been widely observed that there is no single “dominant” SAT solver; instead, different solvers perform
best on different instances.
Objectives: Rather than following the traditional approach of choosing the best solver for a given class of SAT instances, we
aim to make this decision fully automatically, online and on a per-instance basis, with the goal of solving a broad range of
SAT instances more efficiently in terms of running time.
Methods: We describe SATzilla, an automated approach for constructing per-instance algorithm portfolios for SAT that use
so-called empirical hardness models to choose among their constituent solvers. This approach takes as input a distribution of
problem instances and a set of component solvers, and constructs a portfolio optimizing a given objective function (such as
mean running time, percent of instances solved, or score in a competition). In this article, we go well beyond earlier versions
of SATzilla, by making the portfolio construction scalable and completely automated, and improving it by integrating local
search solvers as candidate solvers, by predicting performance score instead of running time, and by using hierarchical
hardness models that take into account different types of SAT instances.
Results: The excellent performance of SATzilla was independently verified in the 2007 SAT Competition, where our SATzilla07
solvers won three gold, one silver and one bronze medal. We demonstrate the effectiveness of the new techniques introduced
here in extensive experimental results on data sets including instances from the most recent SAT competition.
Conclusions: The effectiveness of the SATzilla approach demonstrated in this article suggests that per-instance automated
algorithm selection may also be possible for NP-hard problems other than SAT. We expect this to pave the way for achieving
substantial improvements in the state of the art in solving other important problems in AI and beyond.

      2.4       When Does Transparency Matter?
300
301   Choosing a program rather than an action can alter incentives because a change in one player’s program may
302   induce a change in her opponent’s response. Suppose, however, that a player can change what her program does
303   without changing what her opponent observes. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, for example, replacing a cooperative
304   program with an indistinguishable program that defects would leave the opponent’s cooperation unchanged,
305   making the deviation profitable. We say that a player has undetectable deviations if, for every program she might
306   submit and every input belief it might receive, she can obtain any desired action by substituting a program
307   that her opponent cannot distinguish from the original. Against a fixed opponent’s program, she can therefore
308   change her own action while holding the opponent’s action fixed, just as in the base game. In this sense, rational
309   behaviour in the meta game 𝐺 𝜏 can differ from rational behaviour in the base game 𝐺 only insofar as 𝜏 reveals
310   something about how programs behave.

      3.2     The mathematics behind the outcomes
434
435   With the formal system in place, programs can reason about their opponent’s behavior using the formalism
436   defined by 𝑆. The system alone, however, does not tell us much about the actual reasoning, only it’s language. We
437   now present some of the mathematical tools underlying the proofs; partly to show how reasoning in 𝑆 actually
438   proceeds, and partly because the results themselves are worth seeing. The same tools also yield results beyond
439   those of Critch et al. (2022): they settle several of their open problems and refute one of their examples. In what
440   follows, we state the general ideas and leave the precise definitions, outcomes and proofs in Appendix B.
441      A fundamental property in our system 𝑆 is that proved statements are true, i.e that 𝑆 is sound (Proposition B.1).
442   Soundness yields both positive and negative results: positively, it shows that DupocBot is unexploitable (Proposi-
443   tion B.3; see also the discussion in Example ref); negatively, since 𝑆 proves only true statements, a false hypothesis
444   has no proof of any length, so any search for one fails (e.g. Proposition B.2).
445      Complementary to soundness, we have completeness: if a statement is true for any model of 𝑆, then 𝑆 should
446   be able to prove it (Theorem B.4 and Corollary B.5). This gives us for instance that DupocBot and CooperateBot
447   mutually cooperate (Proposition B.6).
448      Soundness and completeness, however, both presuppose that we can judge whether a hypothesis is true inde-
449   pendently of the search for its proof. This fails when programs reason about each other, e.g., when DupocBot(𝑘)
450   faces itself, its hypothesis is true only if its own search succeeds (example 2.6). Such self-fulfilling hypotheses are
451   resolved by Löb’s theorem (Löb 1955), a core theorem (Critch 2019) in mathematical logic at the heart of Critch
452   et al. (2022)’s results. We state it as Theorem B.7 and re-derive the surprising result of DupocBot’s self cooperation
453   (Theorem B.8). Beyond self-cooperation, Löb’s theorem resolves several of Critch et al.’s open problems and
454   refutes their Example 5.3(a) (Appendix B.3).

647   4.1.3 The formal system 𝑆. Perhaps the most interesting mechanization result is the actual formalization of 𝑆, a
648   language of arithmetic with two action constants, Peano Arithmetic with the action axiom, and proofs measured
649   by their number of symbols. In lean, we build 𝑆 on top of the Foundation library. We sketch the mechanization
650   here and leave further details in appendix C.

652   only 0, 1, +, ·, < and the two action constants. For 𝑆 to reason about sentences, proofs and programs, each of
653   these objects is assigned a natural number, its Gödel code. A statement about proofs or programs then becomes a
654   statement about numbers, which 𝑆 can express.

668   4.2        What 𝑆 Does Not Decide
669   The tools of section 3.2 show us how to settle certain types of hypotheses of our proof_search function. However,
670   one kind of hypothesis escapes them: one that is true, but true only because some other search fails. Whether such
671   a hypothesis is found is a question the tools cannot answer, and one that 𝑆 itself leaves open. This subsection
672   explains why, and states what we prove instead.
673      A search of budget 𝑘 fails when no proof of length at most 𝑘 exists. One might expect this failure to be an easy
674   fact, provable within the same budget 𝑘. To see why it is not, suppose any failed 𝑘-search could be certified within
675   budget 𝑘: for any hypothesis 𝜑 with else-action 𝑏, ⊬𝑘 𝜑 implies ⊢𝑘 (me plays 𝑏). Then we obtain a contradiction
676   with the following anti-diagonal program18 :
677
        -- "If I can prove that I defect against myself, cooperate"
678
        def AntiBot (k : Nat) : Prog :=
679       .search k
680         (.plays .self .self Action.D)      -- hypothesis: I play D against myself
681         (.const Action.C)                  -- proof found: cooperate
682         (.const Action.D)                  -- otherwise: defect
683
        A certificate that a search failed may therefore cost more than the search itself, and nothing in 𝑆 says in general
684
      how much more. This matters as soon as one program’s hypothesis depends on another program’s else play.
685
      Consider CupodTrollBot, which cooperates with any program except if its opponent is literally CupodBot:
686
687     def CupodTrollBot (k : Nat) : Prog :=
688       .search k
689         (.eq .opp (CupodBot k)) -- test if opponent is literally CupodBot
            (.const Action.D)       -- then, defect
690
            (.const Action.C)       -- else, cooperate
691
692   Against DupocBot, its hypothesis is false, so by soundness its search fails and it cooperates, at every budget.
693   DupocBot’s hypothesis, “CupodTrollBot plays 𝐶 against me”, is therefore true. But it is true only because
694   CupodTrollBot’s search failed, and to find it DupocBot needs, within its budget 𝑘, a proof that a search of the
695   same budget 𝑘 fails: exactly the kind of certificate we just showed is not free. None of our tools in the previous
696   section decides whether this certificate fits. What we can prove is that the outcome depends on nothing else.

847   5.1.3 Equilibrium Selection under Replicator Dynamics. Which of these equilibria does a population reach when
848   programs that earn higher payoffs become more common? We study this question using replicator dynamics
849   in 𝐺 prog . Programs are matched at random, and a program’s share grows whenever its expected payoff exceeds
850   the population mean. For the eight-program library Σ at full transparency, every trajectory we simulated from
851   a uniformly drawn initial population converged to the fully cooperative component (Appendix E.2). Thus,
852   although mutual defection remains an equilibrium outcome, in every run the dynamics converge to a cooperative
853   equilibrium. The initial composition influenced which programs persisted in the long run. In some populations
854   only DupocBot was played at equilibrium, while in most it coexisted with both CooperateBot and TitForTatBot.
855   Every pairing among these three programs yields mutual cooperation, so each earns 𝑅 = 2 regardless of its
856   population share. Thus, under full transparency, all sampled populations reached the same cooperative outcome
857   through different mixtures of programs. A positive share of population of DupocBot is required for cooperation
858   in equilibrium.

1237      Proposition B.1 (Soundness). Every sentence provable in 𝑆 is true in N under the standard reading. In particular,
1238   ⊢𝑘 𝜑 implies |= 𝜑, for every budget 𝑘.
1239
1240      Proof. N satisfies the axioms of Peano Arithmetic, and under the standard reading, where 𝑐𝐶 is 0 and 𝑐 𝐷 is
1241   1, it satisfies the action axiom. So N is a model of 𝑇Act , and every sentence a theory proves holds in each of its
1242   models (Soundness of first-order theories; Enderton, 2001, Section 2.5).                                         □
1243
1244       Soundness allows us to prove that certain searches must fail, through its contrapositive:
1245
1246
           Proposition B.2. Let DefectBot be the program that always defects. Then for every 𝑘,
1247
                                                  outcome(DupocBot(𝑘), DefectBot) = (𝐷, 𝐷)
1248
1249      Proof. DefectBot plays 𝐷, so the hypothesis “DefectBot cooperates with me” is false. By the contrapositive
